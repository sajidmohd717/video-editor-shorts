# Who Is Actually Paying for the AI Boom?

Every few weeks, another number. A hundred billion dollars. Three hundred billion. Six hundred and thirty-eight billion.

The numbers are real. They're in press releases and regulatory filings, announced by some of the largest companies on earth.

But there's a question almost nobody asks about them, and it isn't whether they're too big. It's simpler than that. Where did the money come from?

Not who announced it. Not what it buys. Who actually paid, and whose hands has it passed through on the way.

Because when you follow a few of these numbers back to their source, something strange happens. They start to lead to each other.

Start with the biggest one.

In September twenty twenty-five, OpenAI and NVIDIA announced a partnership to deploy at least ten gigawatts of NVIDIA systems. The reporting was immediate and enormous. NVIDIA was investing a hundred billion dollars in OpenAI.

Here's what the announcement actually says.

It's a letter of intent. NVIDIA intends to invest up to a hundred billion dollars. And the money is released progressively, as each gigawatt is deployed.

Four qualifiers in one sentence. A letter of intent isn't a contract. Intends to isn't has. Up to isn't will. And progressively as deployed means almost none of it moves on day one.

None of that is hidden. It's in the first paragraph, on the company's own website.

But the number that travelled was a hundred billion dollars. And that number went into headlines, into valuations, and into other people's business plans.

Now hold onto the shape of that, because you're going to see it again.

Microsoft and NVIDIA both announced investments in Anthropic. The coverage described it as roughly fifteen billion dollars.

Anthropic's own announcement says NVIDIA is committing up to ten billion, and Microsoft up to five billion. Two separate commitments, both capped, neither described as money that has already moved.

And in the same announcement, Anthropic commits to purchase thirty billion dollars of Microsoft Azure compute.

So Microsoft commits up to five billion dollars to a company, and that company commits thirty billion dollars back to Microsoft.

I want to be careful here, because this is where it would be easy to be unfair. That is not fraud. It isn't hidden. It's a normal thing that capital-intensive industries do, and we'll come back to why serious people think it's the right call.

But notice what it does to a number. When Microsoft reports Azure growth, some part of that growth was purchased by a customer that Microsoft is helping to fund.

The revenue is real. What it tells you about demand is a harder question.

Which brings us to the number that made me want to make this video.

In June twenty twenty-six, Oracle reported remaining performance obligations of six hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars. Up three hundred and sixty-three percent in a year.

Remaining performance obligations is a real accounting term. It means contracted work not yet delivered. It is a backlog. Not revenue, not cash. It's a promise to pay, in the future, for something not yet built.

Then there's this line, in Oracle's own release.

The prepaid and customer supplied hardware portions of our large AI contracts now total seventy-five billion dollars.

Read that again. Of that backlog, seventy-five billion dollars was either paid up front by customers, or was hardware the customers bought and handed to Oracle themselves.

That's Oracle telling you, in its own earnings release, that a large part of its record backlog was funded by the buyer.

I don't think that's scandalous. I think it's the clearest window anyone has given us into how this is actually being financed. And it's sitting in a public document that almost nobody read.

Here's the strongest version of the other side, and it deserves saying properly.

One side has enormous capital and wants to sell compute. The other side is confident about future revenue but doesn't have billions in cash today. Financing your customer is how capital-intensive industries have always been built. Railways, telecoms, aircraft leasing. If the demand is real, this is simply the fastest way to build for it.

That argument is not stupid. It might well be right.

But vendor financing has a known failure mode, and it isn't subtle. It works beautifully while demand grows. And when demand disappoints, the vendor loses twice. The sale, and the investment they made to create it.

So rather than predicting a crash, which I can't do and neither can anyone selling you one, here's what I'd actually watch.

One. Whether the up to numbers convert into money that actually moves. Commitments can be quietly restructured, and they have been before.

Two. Whether backlog becomes delivered revenue on schedule. A backlog is a promise. Promises have dates.

Three. Whether anyone in this loop starts buying compute with cash they earned from customers outside the loop.

That third one is the whole question, really.

The AI boom is real. The technology works. But a meaningful share of the money announced so far has been moving between a small number of companies who are each other's investors, customers and suppliers. And the headline figures are consistently larger than the money that has actually changed hands.

Whether that's the start of something enormous, or the sound of a circle closing, the honest answer is that we don't know yet.

But now you know which numbers to watch.
