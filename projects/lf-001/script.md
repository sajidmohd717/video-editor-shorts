## CH1 — The question (0:00–1:30)

`[COLD OPEN: news montage, source audio, 3-4 clips, ~8s total. Anchors saying
the numbers out loud. Chyrons visible. Per L5, this is the one place source
audio leads.]`

Every few weeks, another number.

A hundred billion dollars. Three hundred billion. Six hundred and thirty-eight
billion.

`[Numbers appear as they're said. Serif on black, per L001's card style.]`

The numbers are real. They're in press releases and regulatory filings, announced
by some of the largest companies on earth.

But there's a question almost nobody asks about them, and it isn't whether
they're too big.

It's simpler than that. **Where did the money come from?**

Not who announced it. Not what it buys. Who actually paid — and whose hands has
it passed through on the way.

`[Beat. Hold on black.]`

Because when you follow a few of these numbers back to their source, something
strange happens. They start to lead to each other.

## CH2 — One deal, read carefully (1:30–3:15)

Start with the biggest one.

`[Screenshot: OpenAI's announcement page, 22 Sept 2025. Highlight sweep on the
key phrases as they're spoken.]`

In September 2025, OpenAI and NVIDIA announced a partnership to deploy at least
ten gigawatts of NVIDIA systems. The reporting was immediate and enormous:
NVIDIA was investing a hundred billion dollars in OpenAI.

Here's what the announcement actually says.

`[Highlight, phrase by phrase.]`

It's a letter of intent. NVIDIA *intends* to invest **up to** a hundred billion
dollars. And the money is released **progressively, as each gigawatt is
deployed.**

Four qualifiers in one sentence. A letter of intent isn't a contract. "Intends
to" isn't "has". "Up to" isn't "will". And "progressively as deployed" means
almost none of it moves on day one.

None of that is hidden. It's in the first paragraph, on the company's own
website.

But the number that travelled was a hundred billion dollars — and that number
went into headlines, into valuations, and into other people's business plans.

`[Hold. This is the thesis landing — no cutting through this line.]`

Now hold onto the shape of that, because you're going to see it again.

## CH3 — The same shape, three times (3:15–5:00)

`[Anthropic's own announcement, highlighted.]`

Microsoft and NVIDIA both announced investments in Anthropic. The coverage
described it as roughly fifteen billion dollars.

Anthropic's own announcement says NVIDIA is committing **up to** ten billion, and
Microsoft **up to** five billion. Two separate commitments, both capped, neither
described as money that has already moved.

And in the same announcement, Anthropic commits to purchase **thirty billion
dollars** of Microsoft Azure compute.

`[Simple diagram: arrows. Investment in. Compute purchase out. Same two
companies.]`

So Microsoft commits up to five billion dollars to a company, and that company
commits thirty billion dollars back to Microsoft.

I want to be careful here, because this is where it would be easy to be unfair.
That is not fraud. It isn't hidden. It's a normal thing that capital-intensive
industries do, and we'll come back to why serious people think it's the right
call.

But notice what it does to a number. When Microsoft reports Azure growth, some
part of that growth was purchased by a customer that Microsoft is helping to
fund.

The revenue is real. What it tells you about *demand* is a harder question.

## CH4 — What the filings say (5:00–6:45)

`[Oracle Q4 FY2026 release. This is the strongest evidence in the video — give
it room. Slower cutting.]`

Which brings us to the number that made me want to make this video.

In June 2026, Oracle reported remaining performance obligations of **six hundred
and thirty-eight billion dollars.** Up three hundred and sixty-three percent in a
year.

`[Chart: RPO over time. Vertical.]`

Remaining performance obligations is a real accounting term. It means contracted
work not yet delivered. It is a backlog — not revenue, not cash. It's a promise
to pay, in the future, for something not yet built.

Then there's this line, in Oracle's own release:

`[Full-screen quote card. White serif on black. Hold.]`

> The prepaid and customer supplied hardware portions of our large AI contracts
> now total $75 billion.

Read that again. Of that backlog, seventy-five billion dollars was either paid up
front by customers — or was hardware the customers bought and handed to Oracle
themselves.

`[Beat.]`

That's Oracle telling you, in its own earnings release, that a large part of its
record backlog was funded by the buyer.

I don't think that's scandalous. I think it's the clearest window anyone has
given us into how this is actually being financed — and it's sitting in a public
document that almost nobody read.

## CH5 — Why smart people are doing this, and what would tell us (6:45–8:15)

Here's the strongest version of the other side, and it deserves saying properly.

`[Interview/keynote footage of executives making this case. Source audio for one
short clip — hearing them say it matters.]`

One side has enormous capital and wants to sell compute. The other side is
confident about future revenue but doesn't have billions in cash today. Financing
your customer is how capital-intensive industries have always been built —
railways, telecoms, aircraft leasing. If the demand is real, this is simply the
fastest way to build for it.

That argument is not stupid. It might well be right.

But vendor financing has a known failure mode, and it isn't subtle. It works
beautifully while demand grows. And when demand disappoints, the vendor loses
twice — the sale, and the investment they made to create it.

`[Archival: telecoms, late 1990s. Brief.]`

So rather than predicting a crash, which I can't do and neither can anyone
selling you one, here's what I'd actually watch.

`[Card: three items, appearing as spoken.]`

**One.** Whether the "up to" numbers convert into money that actually moves.
Commitments can be quietly restructured, and they have been before.

**Two.** Whether backlog becomes delivered revenue on schedule. RPO is a promise.
Promises have dates.

**Three.** Whether anyone in this loop starts buying compute with cash they
earned from customers outside the loop.

`[Final card. Hold to the end.]`

That third one is the whole question, really.

The AI boom is real. The technology works. But a meaningful share of the money
announced so far has been moving between a small number of companies who are each
other's investors, customers and suppliers — and the headline figures are
consistently larger than the money that has actually changed hands.

Whether that's the start of something enormous or the sound of a circle closing,
the honest answer is: we don't know yet.

But now you know which numbers to watch.
